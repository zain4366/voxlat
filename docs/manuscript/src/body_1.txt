# INTRODUCTION {#sec:intro}

Electric machines for aircraft, rotorcraft, drones and road vehicles are limited by heat. Copper and iron losses in the stator must leave through a thin housing, and the hottest point of the winding insulation, not the average temperature, sets the continuous rating [@vanheerden2022; @gai2019]. Liquid jackets around the stator are the usual answer. They are judged by three numbers: the thermal resistance between stator and coolant, the pumping power, and the mass. The third matters most in flight, where every kilogram of thermal hardware costs payload or range [@vanheerden2022].

Laser powder-bed fusion makes it possible to fill the jacket gap with a lattice instead of a channel. Network (skeletal) triply periodic minimal surface (TPMS) lattices such as the gyroid and the Schwarz diamond are attractive for this purpose: their solid and fluid phases are both continuous, they are self-supporting, their surface area per volume is large, and the same struts that exchange heat can also carry the motor torque and thrust from the sleeve to the housing [@alketan2019; @alketan2018]. TPMS heat exchangers and heat sinks are now studied widely [@brambati2024; @you2024; @hassanirad2027; @ma2026], including a gyroid housing for an air-cooled motor [@hassan2024]. Recent work grades the lattice in space, varying the density, the cell size or the morphology to steer the flow and to put heat-transfer area where it is needed [@you2024; @hassanirad2027; @kawabe2026].

Designing such a graded lattice for a whole device is expensive. A pore-resolved conjugate simulation of a motor jacket has tens of millions of cells, and an optimizer needs hundreds to thousands of designs. Design studies therefore replace the lattice by a homogenized porous medium whose properties (permeability, inertial coefficient, effective conductivity, interstitial heat-transfer coefficient, stiffness) come from periodic unit-cell simulations [@gajetti2025; @savoldi2026; @modrek2022; @ohtani2025; @kawabe2026]. Periodic closures rest on scale separation: the cell must be small compared with the region it fills [@whitaker1986; @sanchezpalencia1980]. A motor jacket breaks this assumption. Its gap is a few millimetres, the smallest printable cells are about 2 mm, and so only one to three cells fit between the sleeve and the outer wall. Wall effects of this kind are well known for packed beds, where the porosity rises near a wall and the flow channels along it [@benenati1962], and size effects are known for the stiffness of cellular solids [@onck2001]. For TPMS lattices, pore-resolved simulations of wall-bounded channels give inertial coefficients that differ from the periodic values [@rathore2023], but we did not find a study that measures the finite-gap error of the closures used in design, separates it from discretization error, and supplies a correction that a device model can use.

A second gap concerns the comparisons. Graded designs are usually compared with uniform lattices of the same family. Whether grading beats a well-tuned conventional jacket, such as a helical channel, at equal pumping power, and how much of any advantage comes from the manifolds rather than the lattice, is rarely tested. Third, multi-objective optimization of such device models is done with evolutionary algorithms or with Bayesian optimization, but their sample efficiency on the same problem, with the same constraints and seeds, is seldom reported.

This paper addresses these gaps for a concrete reference problem, the liquid jacket of a 15 kW inrunner permanent-magnet motor, and asks three research questions:

- **RQ1.** How large is the error of bulk homogenized properties when only N = 1–8 cells fit across a bounded gap, and can a simple correction remove it?
- **RQ2.** Under identical constraints and at equal pump power, does grading the density, morphology and cell size beat a tuned helical channel, the best uniform gyroid and density-only grading?
- **RQ3.** How sample-efficient is batch Bayesian multi-objective optimization (qNEHVI) compared with NSGA-II and random search on this problem?

The contributions are:

a) verified periodic voxel solvers for the effective conductivity, stiffness and Stokes permeability of gyroid, diamond and blended network lattices, with a two-grid estimator whose error is below 1% for permeability and conductivity and below 3% for stiffness (@sec:cell);
b) a systematic finite-gap study of TPMS closures, the first we are aware of: 336 wall-bounded strips with one to eight cells, several cut phases, density gradients and cell stretches, and a 1/N correction with fitted wall coefficients that removes the permeability error down to the cut-phase noise floor (@sec:rq1);
c) a sub-second homogenized jacket model that couples Darcy–Forchheimer flow, a three-field non-equilibrium energy balance and a sandwich structural check, verified against analytic solutions to round-off (@sec:device);
d) a like-for-like comparison of optimized graded lattices with tuned baselines on two manifold layouts, and a five-seed comparison of NSGA-II, qNEHVI and Sobol search (@sec:rq2, @sec:rq3);
e) a manufacturable optimum exported as a voxel-checked printable geometry, and an open-source, scripted pipeline that reproduces every figure and table on a laptop CPU.

The study is computational. It uses no pore-resolved conjugate CFD of the full jacket and no experiment; the two closures that we could not compute ourselves, the inertial coefficient and the interstitial heat-transfer coefficient, are taken from the literature. @sec:limits states what this means for the conclusions. The rest of the paper is organized as follows. @sec:related reviews related work, @sec:problem defines the reference problem, @sec:methods describes the solvers and models with their verification, @sec:results presents the results for RQ1–RQ3, and @sec:concl concludes.

# RELATED WORK AND RESEARCH GAP {#sec:related}

## Thermal–hydraulic closures for TPMS lattices

Pore-scale simulations of single periodic cells have produced power-law fits of the permeability and the Forchheimer coefficient of skeletal gyroid, diamond and Split-P lattices for porosities of 0.3–0.6 and hydraulic Reynolds numbers up to 100 [@gajetti2025]. The same group derived a modified Reynolds analogy for the interstitial Nusselt number of the gyroid in laminar flow, calibrated at a Prandtl number of one [@savoldi2026]. Rathore et al. simulated four-cell channels of several TPMS types and reported permeabilities and inertial coefficients that include the channel walls [@rathore2023]. Correlations for sheet TPMS heat exchangers in turbulent flow [@brambati2024], for printed gyroid ducts at high Prandtl number [@piandoro2026] and a recent meta-analysis of 57 studies [@alsafadi2026] complete the picture. Most closures are bulk properties of a single cell; the effect of a bounding wall is either absent or folded into a device-specific correlation.

## Homogenization-based design of graded lattices

Homogenization lets a lattice be designed as a continuum whose local parameters vary in space, followed by a de-homogenization step that builds the actual geometry. Modrek et al. optimized conductive heat sinks with homogenized gyroid conductivities and mapped the result to gyroid sheets and solids [@modrek2022]. Ohtani et al. optimized the wall thickness of a two-fluid gyroid heat exchanger with a Brinkman–Forchheimer porous model and validated the result with full CFD [@ohtani2025]. Kawabe et al. graded the cell size of gyroid lattices for stiffness and proposed a phase-field de-homogenization that keeps cells undistorted [@kawabe2026]. Andreassen and Andreasen give the standard numerical homogenization procedure [@andreassen2014]. All of these use bulk closures, which presume many cells across every feature.

## Finite-size and wall effects

Near a flat wall, a random packing of spheres has a porosity close to one within half a diameter, which creates a fast channel along the wall [@benenati1962]. Brinkman's equation [@brinkman1949; @neale1974] is the usual continuum description of such boundary layers. The stiffness of a few-cell cellular solid differs from the bulk value because cut cells at the free surface carry less load [@onck2001]. A TPMS lattice bonded to two walls is a different case: the cut is periodic, not random, and the bond can stiffen rather than weaken the boundary cells. How the closures of such a lattice change with the number of cells across a gap has, to our knowledge, not been quantified.

## Motor cooling jackets and optimizers

Conventional stator jackets use spiral, axial or U-shaped channels, often optimized with CFD-trained surrogates [@gai2019; @li2026]. Multi-objective problems of this kind are commonly solved with NSGA-II [@deb2002; @blank2020]. Batch Bayesian optimization with expected-hypervolume acquisition (qEHVI, qNEHVI) promises far fewer evaluations [@emmerich2006; @daulton2021; @balandat2020], at a cost in wall-clock time for fitting and acquisition. On the same expensive engineering problem, the two are rarely compared with several seeds, a common budget unit and identical constraints.

# REFERENCE PROBLEM {#sec:problem}

The demonstrator is the cooling jacket of a 15 kW continuous inrunner permanent-magnet synchronous motor with an efficiency of 0.94 (@tbl:ref). Of the 957 W of total loss, 770 W are assumed to enter the jacket through the stator sleeve (80%); the rest leaves through the end windings, rotor and shaft. The aluminium sleeve (inner radius 70 mm, thickness 1.5 mm) is surrounded by a 6 mm lattice gap (r = 71.5–77.5 mm) and a 2 mm outer wall, over an axial length of 50 mm. The wall heat flux on the stator side has a mean of 35.0 kW m$^{-2}$ and an end-peaked profile,

$$ \begin{aligned} &q''(z) = \bar q\left[1 + 0.25\left(12\left(\zeta - \tfrac{1}{2}\right)^2 - 1\right)\right], \\ &\zeta = z/L_{ax}, \qquad \bar q = Q/\left(2\pi r_s L_{ax}\right) \end{aligned} $$ {#eq:qwall}

so that the axial ends see twice the centre value. A 50/50 water–glycol coolant enters at 50 °C with a nominal flow of 3 L/min (swept from 1 to 5 L/min). In the reference layout the coolant enters through an axial manifold slot at θ = 0°, splits into two half-annulus paths and leaves through a second slot at θ = 180°; both slots are 15 mm wide and span the full length. An alternative end-ring layout, with ring manifolds at the two axial ends outside the heated length, is studied in @sec:rq2.

The lattice and walls are AlSi10Mg. The lattice must carry the motor torque (36 N m) and thrust (780 N) from the sleeve to the housing and hold a coolant pressure of 2 bar gauge; the allowable stress of 80 MPa is a fatigue-limited assumption. Manufacturability requires a minimum wall of 0.35 mm, a minimum pore throat of 0.8 mm (powder removal) and a limited density change per cell. The three objectives are the thermal resistance $R = (T_{wall,max} - T_{in})/Q$, with $T_{wall,max}$ the hottest point on the stator side of the sleeve; the pump power $P = \Delta p\,\dot V/\eta_{pump}$ with $\eta_{pump} = 0.3$; and the jacket mass (lattice, sleeve and outer wall). All values are stored in one configuration file of the software (`configs/reference.yaml`).
