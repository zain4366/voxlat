# RESULTS AND DISCUSSION {#sec:results}

## Bulk closures of gyroid, diamond and blends {#sec:bulk}

The bulk closures set the scale for everything that follows (@fig:bulkK). At ρ* = 0.35 the conductivity of the gyroid and the diamond is $k_{eff}/k_s$ = 0.208 and 0.205, 67–83% of the Hashin–Shtrikman upper bound over the design range, with power-law fits $0.81\rho^{*1.29}$ (G) and $0.85\rho^{*1.36}$ (D); the coolant adds only 1–4%. The Young's modulus scales as $E^*/E_s = 0.90\rho^{*2.13}$ (G) and $0.67\rho^{*1.93}$ (D), the exponent of about two of bending-dominated lattices [@khaderi2014; @alketan2018]. With the exponent fixed at two, the prefactors (0.79 for G, 0.72 for D) are 11% and 9% above the values measured by Maskery et al. on polymer lattices [@maskery2018], a plausible gap between an ideal geometry and printed parts with porosity and roughness. Neither lattice is elastically isotropic: the Zener ratio is 1.3–1.9 for G and 1.7–2.3 for D, so the device model uses full tensors. The permeability follows $K/L^2 = 0.0202\,\phi^{3.22}$ (G) and $0.0132\,\phi^{3.29}$ (D); the diamond is 35–37% less permeable at equal porosity because its pores and throats are smaller. The Kozeny constant $c_K = \phi^3/(K a_{sf}^2)$ collapses within about 4% for all three morphologies at ϕ ≥ 0.5, falling from about 8 to 6 between ϕ = 0.8 and 0.5.

![fig:bulkK|full](figs/task4_permeability_vs_porosity.png) Bulk permeability of the network lattices from the R(32, 64) estimator: (a) K/L² against porosity for gyroid, diamond and the 50/50 blend (band = range of its principal values), (b) effective Kozeny constant φ³/(K a_sf²), (c) hydraulic tortuosity.

The blends are different. At w = 0.5 and ρ* below about 0.375, the pinch-point necks of @sec:geom make the lattice 3.4–4.5 times softer than G or D, strongly anisotropic, and give it 2.5–4 times higher stress localization (99th-percentile factor up to 95 at ρ* = 0.2). Between ρ* = 0.35 and 0.40 the Young's modulus jumps threefold and the conductivity rises from about 40% to 60% of the upper bound, the signature of a topology change. The blend permeability, in contrast, stays close to that of the gyroid. Blends therefore trade structure for flow, and their properties change abruptly with the parameters, which is why the surrogate errors concentrate there (@sec:surr).

## RQ1: finite-gap error and its correction {#sec:rq1}

@fig:gap and @tbl:delta give the discrepancy of the five core properties as a function of the number of cells across the gap. The dominant effect is on the in-plane permeability. The bulk closure over-predicts $K_t$ by 23–40% at N = 1 ($\delta$ = −19 to −28%), 10–17% at N = 2 and 7–10% at N = 3, which is the whole jacket design range (L = 2–6 mm in a 6 mm gap). The deficit is cleanly proportional to 1/N: a $1/N^2$ term does not improve the fit, the root-mean-square deviation from the 1/N line is 0.4 pp, and the wall coefficient hardly depends on density or on where the wall cuts the lattice (phase standard deviation of $a_K$ ≤ 0.011 for G and D). The wall coefficient is $a_K$ = 0.25–0.28 for the gyroid and 0.19–0.22 for the diamond at ρ* = 0.25–0.45 (@tbl:delta), and grows with axial stretch (0.24/0.27/0.30 for G at $a_z$ = 0.75/1/1.5). In the parallel picture of Eq. (@eq:gappar), each wall carries a stagnant layer of thickness $a_K L/2$ ≈ 0.13 L (G), 0.10 L (D) and 0.12 L (blend).

![fig:gap|full](figs/task7_error_vs_N.png) RQ1: discrepancy δ = q_strip/q_bulk − 1 of the bulk closure against 1/N for lattice strips between bonded walls (n = 32 voxels per cell): (a, f) through-gap conductivity, (b, g) in-plane permeability, (c, h) normal stiffness, (d, i) transverse shear G_t that carries torque and thrust, (e, j) in-plane shear G_sz. Top row: gyroid (blue) and diamond (orange); bottom row: w = 0.5 blend. Markers = mean over symmetry-distinct cut phases, bars = their range, lines = fitted 1/N model, shaded band = jacket design range (N = 1–3).


The mechanism is not near-wall channelling (@fig:velslices). For a randomly packed bed, porosity rises toward one at the wall and a fast layer forms [@benenati1962]. For a TPMS lattice the planar porosity at a cut equals the bulk porosity, the wall closes the pores it cuts, and the flow deficit is confined to about a quarter of a cell next to each wall; the interior velocity profile equals the bulk one. A Brinkman description with the fluid viscosity as effective viscosity [@brinkman1949; @neale1974] gives a boundary layer of thickness $\sqrt K$ (2√K/L = 0.11–0.14 at ρ* = 0.35) and under-predicts the loss by a factor of about 1.9. Moreover, the measured coefficient scales with L at fixed density rather than with √K, so a Brinkman layer is the wrong model here.

![fig:velslices|full](figs/task7_velocity_slices.png) Flow in a finite-gap strip (Stokes, unit body force, N = 2, n = 48; grey = solid): (a) axial velocity in a slice of a gyroid strip at ρ* = 0.35, (b) plane-averaged velocity profile of the strip (colour) against the bulk cell with the same cut (grey). The deficit is confined to about L/4 next to each wall; there is no fast near-wall channel.

The through-gap conductivity is almost unaffected for G and D (|δ| ≤ 2.3%, below the surrogate error), because the constriction of heat flow at the strut–wall contacts and the spreading in the wall nearly cancel. The walls stiffen the core: at N = 1, $C_{nn}$ rises by 2–7%, $G_{sz}$ by 2–11%, and $G_t$ by 2–7% for the gyroid, while it falls by 2–4% for the diamond. For the blends below ρ* ≈ 0.4, the stiffness gains reach +50 to +190% at N = 1 because a wall replaces the weakest pinch necks; these effects depend strongly on the cut phase and do not follow 1/N.

Can a simple correction remove the error? @tbl:gapcv answers this. For G and D the physical 1/N model reduces the cross-validated permeability error from 12.9 pp to 0.40 pp, close to the floor of 0.28 pp set by the cut phase, which a homogenized model cannot know; leave-one-N-out validation gives the same result (13.4 → 0.29 pp), so the form also extrapolates in N. A Gaussian process on the residuals never improves the error by more than 0.1 pp and sometimes worsens it. For the blends the 1/N model still removes most of the permeability error (12.9 → 3.5 pp), but their stiffness corrections are dominated by the cut phase and are not predictable from $(N,\theta)$ at all. The answer to RQ1 is therefore: for G and D the finite-gap error is large only for the permeability, and the one-coefficient correction $K_t = K_{bulk}(1 - a_K/N)$, with $a_K \approx 0.27$ (G) and 0.20 (D) at ρ* = 0.35, removes it to within the cut-phase noise.

Density gradients across the gap add a second, smaller error, because a local Darcy closure cannot see the momentum exchange between fast low-density and slow high-density layers. Relative to uniform strips with the same mean density, the worst-case change of any G/D property is 0.9% at g = |∇ρ*|L = 0.025, 1.0% at 0.05, 2.0% at 0.075 and 3.5% at 0.15 (Supplementary Fig. S23). This confirms the gradient limit of 0.05 used in the optimization. The study is radial; the design grades in-plane, where the same per-cell gradient is expected to bound the error. For blends the gradient effect is −8 to −20% even at small g, so no gradient limit makes graded blends safe.

At device level the correction matters mainly for the pump power. For the uniform reference gyroid (@sec:device_results), omitting it lowers the predicted pressure drop by 6.7% but changes R by only 0.1%, because the hottest point of the slot layout lies outside the lattice. Without the correction, the viscous (Darcy) part of the pressure drop of a G or D lattice is underestimated by 7–37% over N = 1–3 (the range of $1/f_K - 1$), which biases any comparison with channel jackets in favour of the lattice.

## Device-level behaviour of the reference jacket {#sec:device_results}

@fig:fields shows the uniform reference gyroid (ρ* = 0.35, L = 4 mm) at 3 L/min. The coolant splits evenly into the two half-annulus paths and heats up by 4.27 K. The pressure drop is 1833 Pa (inertia carries 62% of it, Forchheimer number 1.65), the pump power 0.31 W, the jacket mass 0.344 kg (lattice 0.123 kg) and the minimum structural margin 12.7. The thermal resistance is R = 24.4 mK W$^{-1}$ ($T_{wall,max}$ = 68.8 °C). The hottest point does not lie in the lattice but on the unfinned sleeve under the outlet slot, at the axial end where the heat flux peaks; the hottest lattice-covered point is 5.9 K cooler. The overall conductance of the lattice region is high ($U_{eff}$ = 12.5 kW m$^{-2}$ K$^{-1}$, fin efficiency 0.45), so the slot strip, which is cooled only by conduction along the sleeve and by the weak plenum coefficient, controls R.

Grey bands = manifold slots; the hottest point (circle) lies on the unfinned sleeve under the outlet slot.

## RQ2: optimized graded lattices against the baselines {#sec:rq2}

**Baselines.** @tbl:baselines lists the tuned baselines. The helical channel B1 has the lowest resistance of all baselines, 13.3 mK W$^{-1}$ at 0.287 W: its end-ring manifolds lie outside the heated length, so the whole sleeve is finned, and the Dean flow in the curved channels raises its Nusselt number from 5.0 (straight duct) to 11.6. With the same axial slots as the lattice, the channel jacket B1s is 5% worse than the uniform gyroid B2 in R and 9% worse in $R_{active}$, at lower pump power, and its family saturates: many short parallel channels cannot convert more pressure drop into heat transfer. Density-only grading B3 is 7% (R) and 11% ($R_{active}$) better than B2 at equal pump power. It densifies the lattice toward the outlet slot, where the density reaches its upper bound of 0.50.

**What the grading does.** @fig:designs shows the representative designs with slots. The design at the reference pump power is a gyroid whose density rises from 0.20 near the inlet to 0.41 at the outlet slot, and whose cell size falls from 5.0 mm to 2.2 mm along each path. Coarse, open cells near the inlet spend little pressure; dense, fine cells near the outlet slot, where the coolant is warmest and the unfinned strip must be fed by sleeve conduction, add surface area and solid conductance. Cell-size grading is the lever that density-only grading B3 lacks; it explains most of the 3.3% gain over B3 and the larger gains in $R_{active}$ (14.4 vs 15.0 mK W$^{-1}$). The structural margin is at least 8.7 for every representative design, so the structural constraint is never active at the reference loads; it would bind only for low-density blends or much higher loads.

![fig:designs|full](figs/task11_designs_slots.png) Representative slot-manifold designs from the combined front: minimum R, best at the 0.306 W cap, knee and minimum mass (rows); relative density, blend weight w (0 = gyroid, 1 = diamond), cell size L and stator-side sleeve temperature (columns) on the unwrapped jacket; circle = hottest point. The best-at-cap design shown carries small blend weights (w ≤ 0.24); its manufacturable version used in this paper has w ≡ 0 with identical density and cell-size fields (@sec:export_results).

**The role of morphology blending.** Under the strict minimum-wall criterion, gyroid–diamond transitions must cross the pinch-neck valleys of @sec:geom, where the minimum wall vanishes. Manufacturable designs are therefore near-pure gyroid or near-pure diamond with graded density and cell size; both families appear on the fronts (near-diamond at minimum R, near-gyroid in the trade-off region), but not as graded mixtures. Grading the morphology in the sense of a continuous blend is thus not useful for this jacket under strict-wall manufacturability. A relaxed criterion that checks only load-path necks would reopen the blends; we leave this to a sensitivity study, since the blends are also structurally weak (@sec:bulk).

**Manifolds dominate.** With end-ring manifolds, the same optimization reaches R = 8.57 mK W$^{-1}$ at only 0.065 W, 35.3% better than the tuned helical channel B1 at its 0.287 W (and 53.5% better at a 0.1 W cap). Here the optimum above about 0.06 W is a uniform dense diamond, and grading pays only at very low pump power (−4 to −11% relative to uniform lattices at ≤ 0.03 W, where the knee design is a light gyroid with coarse cells at the inlet ring and fine cells at the outlet ring). The answer to RQ2 is therefore two-sided. Under identical constraints and manifolds, grading density and cell size beats every baseline at equal pump power, by 3–15% depending on the baseline. The choice of manifolds changes R by a factor of 2.6, far more than any lattice grading.

## RQ3: sample efficiency of the optimizers {#sec:rq3}

@fig:hv and @tbl:hv compare the hypervolume reached by the three methods. On both layouts qNEHVI pulls ahead after 30–40 evaluations and reaches a median hypervolume at 200 evaluations that NSGA-II needs a median of 1368 evaluations to match. Bayesian optimization is therefore about seven times more sample-efficient. Sobol search never reaches it within 1000 evaluations. With its full budget of 10 000 evaluations NSGA-II eventually exceeds qNEHVI's 200-evaluation result (0.209 vs 0.200 with slots, 0.688 vs 0.675 with end rings). The seed-to-seed variation is small (interquartile range ≤ 0.003).

Wall-clock time tells the opposite story. A 200-evaluation qNEHVI run took 1660–3780 s on a four-core machine, 90–95% of it in GP fitting and acquisition over 72 dimensions, whereas NSGA-II reaches the same hypervolume in about 1368 × (0.65–0.85) s ≈ 15–20 min. With a model that costs under one second, the evolutionary algorithm is faster in practice; Bayesian optimization pays off once one evaluation costs more than roughly 10–20 s, as for a three-dimensional conjugate CFD or FE model of the jacket. The anchors matter for every method: without the nine uniform anchor designs, all fronts missed the dense-diamond corner, where a uniform diamond beat them by 5–8% (end rings), and the median slot hypervolume fell to 0.154 (NSGA-II after 2000 evaluations) and 0.196 (qNEHVI after 200). The repair and the anchors are therefore part of the method, not tuning details, and they are applied identically to every optimizer.

## Manufacturable optimum and exported geometry {#sec:export_results}

The direct voxel check of the exported geometry changed one result. The optimizer's best design at the reference pump power carried small blend weights (w ≤ 0.24). In a 30° sector at 0.06 mm it showed 0.28–0.31 mm necks, below the 0.35 mm limit, in the first cell next to the end cap. The cause was twofold: the n = 48 wall table over-reads the strict minimum wall of this blend by about 6% (6–12% for the diamond, from an n = 96 check), more than the 1% margin of the cell-size rule, and near the axial ends the conformal phase field compresses cells circumferentially by 3% and tilts them by up to 9°. Setting w ≡ 0 with unchanged density and cell-size fields gives R = 21.92 mK W$^{-1}$ (−0.09%), P = 0.308 W (+1.2%), the same mass and a margin of 9.8. This pure-gyroid design passes every check and is the optimum reported in @tbl:rq2.
