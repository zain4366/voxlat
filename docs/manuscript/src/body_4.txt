# LIMITATIONS {#sec:limits}

The conclusions above are bounded by the following limitations, in decreasing order of importance.

- **No experiment and no pore-resolved conjugate simulation of the jacket.** Every solver is verified against exact or published solutions (@tbl:verif, @fig:deviceverif), and our permeabilities agree with independent body-fitted CFD to within 2% [@gajetti2025]. Verification is not validation [@oberkampf2010], however: the device-level thermal resistances and pressure drops are predictions of a homogenized model whose closures and assumptions have not been tested together against a measurement. The relative rankings in RQ2 are more robust than the absolute values, because all lattices share the same closures. Even so, the comparison with the channel baselines B1 and B1s rests on duct correlations, while the lattices rest on porous-media closures; B1's advantage in particular depends on a coil correlation for circular tubes applied to a 1.75 mm × 6 mm rectangle (removing the Dean enhancement raises its R by 49%). The natural next step is a pore-resolved conjugate simulation of a 30° sector of the exported geometry, followed by a flow-loop test of a printed jacket.
- **Creeping-flow permeability and the finite-gap correction.** $K$ and the finite-gap factor are Stokes (Re → 0) results. In the jacket, inertia carries 16–84% of the pressure drop. We apply the finite-gap correction to the Darcy term only and use the bulk permeability inside the inertial term; whether the wall layer also changes the inertial coefficient is unknown. The correction was fitted at n = 32 (stiffness discrepancies carry about ±1.5 pp at N = 1), for perfectly bonded flat walls and for ρ* = 0.25–0.45 and $a_z$ = 0.75–1.5.
- **Empirical Nusselt and Forchheimer closures.** The inertial coefficient comes from pore-scale simulations of skeletal gyroid and diamond cells fitted for porosities of 0.3–0.6 and $Re_{D_h} \le 100$ [@gajetti2025]. Our jacket operates at porosities of 0.5–0.8 and $Re_{D_h}$ ≈ 25–580, so pump-power predictions inherit this extrapolation; for the diamond above ϕ = 0.6, plausible values of $C_F$ differ by up to a factor of two. The interstitial heat-transfer coefficient follows the modified Reynolds analogy of Savoldi et al. [@savoldi2026], calibrated for the gyroid at Pr = 1, ϕ ≤ 0.7 and $Re_{D_h}$ = 20–100. We extend it to the water–glycol coolant (Pr ≈ 21) with the Chilton–Colburn factor $Pr^{1/3}$, and to the diamond and the blends through their own friction factors. An exponent of 0.4 would raise $h_{sf}$ by 22%, and the alternative variant of the analogy lowers it by up to 22% inside its fitted range. Both closures come from one research group and one CFD set-up. Removing these assumptions requires experiments or conjugate pore-resolved simulations at the operating Prandtl number.
- **Manifolds.** The slot plenums are well mixed with a laminar plate-channel coefficient $h_m$, to which R is very sensitive (−12% at 1000 and −40% at 5000 W m$^{-2}$ K$^{-1}$). The end-ring layout and B1 both assume ideal ring manifolds outside the heated length with no port losses; since the lattice pressure drop with end rings is only 40–400 Pa, real ring and port losses would be a larger fraction of its pump power than of B1's.
- **Structure and printing.** The structural check is a rigid-face sandwich with bulk 99th-percentile localization factors and linear superposition; it ignores fatigue notch effects at the as-built surface, residual stresses and the finite-gap change of localization. Surface roughness, porosity and the geometric deviations of laser powder-bed fusion are not modelled; they are known to raise the friction factor of printed gyroids [@savoldi2026]. The strict minimum-wall rule excludes graded gyroid–diamond blends, and the surrogates are least accurate in the pinch-neck region (about 10% stiffness error).
- **Scope.** One motor, one gap height, one coolant and one material were studied. The finite-gap coefficients are dimensionless and should transfer to other gaps with the same N, but the device-level gains are specific to this reference problem.

# CONCLUSIONS {#sec:concl}

We built and verified an open-source, laptop-scale pipeline that computes TPMS lattice closures on periodic voxel cells, corrects them for the finite number of cells across a bounded gap, and uses them to optimize a graded liquid-cooling jacket for a 15 kW electric motor. The main conclusions are:

1. **Finite-gap error (RQ1).** With one to three cells across the gap, bulk closures over-predict the in-plane permeability of gyroid and diamond lattices by 7–40%. The deficit is proportional to 1/N, nearly independent of density and cut phase, and caused by a stagnant layer of about 0.10–0.13 L at each bonded wall, not by near-wall channelling. The correction $K_t = K_{bulk}(1 - a_K/N)$ with $a_K \approx 0.27$ (gyroid) and 0.20 (diamond) reduces the cross-validated error from 12.9 to 0.4 pp, the cut-phase noise floor; a Gaussian process on the residuals adds nothing. The through-gap conductivity changes by at most 2.3%, and the bonded walls stiffen the core. A density change of 0.05 per cell keeps the local-closure error below 1%.
2. **Graded designs versus baselines (RQ2).** Under identical constraints and manifolds, grading density and cell size lowers the thermal resistance at equal pump power by 14.6% relative to circumferential channels, 10.1% relative to the best uniform gyroid, 11.1% relative to the best uniform diamond and 3.3% relative to density-only grading, at 10% less mass than the uniform gyroid. The optimizer densifies and refines the lattice toward the outlet slot. Continuous gyroid–diamond blending is not useful under strict-wall manufacturability because blends develop pinch-point necks.
3. **Manifolds dominate.** Replacing the axial slots by end rings lowers the thermal resistance by a factor of 2.6 and turns a 65% deficit relative to a tuned helical channel into a 35% advantage at less than a quarter of its pump power. For lattice jackets, the manifold layout should be designed first.
4. **Optimizers (RQ3).** Batch Bayesian optimization (qNEHVI) reaches in 200 evaluations the hypervolume that NSGA-II needs about 1370 evaluations for, a sevenfold gain in sample efficiency, while NSGA-II is faster in wall-clock time for a sub-second model. Bayesian optimization should be preferred once one evaluation costs more than roughly 10–20 s. A feasibility repair and a common set of uniform anchors are essential for every method.
5. **Manufacturability.** A direct voxel check of the exported geometry caught a minimum-wall violation that the tabulated cell-size rule had missed; the corrected, pure-gyroid optimum passes all wall, throat, connectivity and pocket checks and loses only 0.09% in R.

Future work should validate the device model with a pore-resolved conjugate simulation of a jacket sector and a flow-loop experiment on a printed part, extend the finite-gap study to the inertial and heat-transfer closures, and add the manifold geometry to the design variables.
